Research statement

What makes a good matrix-aware optimizer?

My working view is that optimizer quality is a joint property of geometry, numerical linear algebra, and training dynamics. A useful method must capture the right blockwise structure, apply it through a stable preconditioner, and compute the required matrix functions cheaply enough for real training.

3numerical concerns: accuracy, stability, throughput

Working thesis

Optimizer quality is not determined by the update formula alone.

A matrix-aware optimizer succeeds only when three components align: the statistical or curvature structure encoded by its preconditioner, the numerical quality of the matrix functions used to realize that preconditioner, and the resulting dynamics across optimization steps. This is the lens connecting my theory, numerical, and empirical work.

Theoretical objects

Curvature and gradient statistics must be analyzed together.

A preconditioner changes the local optimization geometry and the stochastic update distribution simultaneously. Studying only one of them can give a misleading account of stability or efficiency.

Block Hessian

Local curvature determines deterministic contraction, stiffness, and the spectral difficulty seen by each parameter block.

Gradient second moment

The uncentered second moment combines mean-gradient signal and stochastic variability, directly matching the statistics accumulated by adaptive methods.

Joint preconditioning

The transformed pair reveals whether a preconditioner improves conditioning while amplifying noise, or suppresses noise while distorting useful curvature directions.

Workflow

From an exact model to a measured training effect.

Step 1

Specify the architecture exactly

Fix tensor orientations, dimensions, normalization placement, attention grouping, positional rotations, residual paths, nonlinearities, and loss.

Step 2

Derive blockwise formulas

Compute Jacobians, Hessian blocks, gradient outer products, and mini-batch decompositions without hiding architecture-dependent terms.

Step 3

Apply the optimizer geometry

Express AdamW and Shampoo transformations consistently and form the corresponding preconditioned curvature and second-moment operators.

Step 4

Control matrix-function error

Track approximation error, damping, staleness, precision, and refresh frequency as parts of the optimizer rather than isolated implementation details.

Step 5

Validate the full dynamics

Measure spectral heterogeneity, convergence, stability, wall-clock time, memory, and sensitivity on Transformer and vision workloads.

Current questions

Questions guiding the work

  • Which architectural operations create the largest blockwise curvature and second-moment disparities?
  • When do AdamW and Shampoo reduce the same heterogeneity, and when do they act on fundamentally different structures?
  • How should curvature and gradient second moments be compared after preconditioning?
  • How much matrix-function approximation error can training tolerate before the optimizer changes qualitatively?
  • Can refresh schedules and damping be controlled from observable proxies rather than fixed heuristics?

Field perspective

The matrix-function bottleneck

Recent work such as DASH and PRISM reinforces a broader point: matrix-aware optimization increasingly depends on GPU-efficient inverse roots and orthogonalization. My interest is in connecting those numerical kernels to the geometry and dynamics they are supposed to preserve.
