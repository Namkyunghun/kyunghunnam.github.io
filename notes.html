Beyond AdamW: Matrix-Aware Optimization and the Matrix-Function Bottleneck

AdamW will remain an important baseline, but the center of gravity in large-model optimization is shifting toward methods that exploit matrix structure rather than treating every parameter coordinate independently.

Kronecker-structured optimizers such as Shampoo and spectral steepest-descent methods such as Muon use information that is discarded by coordinate-wise adaptation. This additional structure can produce better-conditioned or more directionally meaningful updates. It also creates an unavoidable systems cost: the optimizer must evaluate matrix functions repeatedly during training.

The bottleneck is the matrix function

For Shampoo, the central primitive is an inverse matrix root, often implemented through eigendecomposition or an iterative solver. For Muon, the core operation is orthogonalization or polar decomposition, typically realized through SVD-like or Newton–Schulz-style iterations. These are not secondary implementation details. Their accuracy, precision, scaling, batching strategy, and refresh frequency determine whether the intended update direction is actually produced at an acceptable cost.

A matrix-aware optimizer is only as practical as the matrix-function kernel that realizes its geometry.

“What makes a good optimizer?” becomes a systems-and-dynamics question

An optimizer is more than a closed-form update rule. Training is a high-dimensional dynamical process in which the preconditioner, momentum, damping, numerical error, finite precision, and update frequency interact over time. A faster matrix function is useful only when it preserves the optimizer's effective geometry and does not introduce instability or qualitatively different dynamics.

This leads to three coupled questions:

  • How can the required matrix function be computed with fewer expensive synchronizations and better accelerator utilization?
  • How can approximation error be controlled as spectra evolve throughout training?
  • How can the numerical method be changed without disrupting convergence, stability, or the intended normalization of updates?

Why DASH and PRISM matter

DASH ↗ accelerates Shampoo through batched block preconditioning and efficient inverse-root solvers, emphasizing GPU utilization and the role of matrix scaling. PRISM ↗ develops an adaptive framework for matrix functions using polynomial fitting and randomized sketching, and applies it to primitives used by both Shampoo and Muon.

These works attack the core operation itself. My work on FOAM approaches the same bottleneck from a complementary direction: rather than replacing eigendecomposition, FOAM controls how accurately and how frequently the preconditioner must be refreshed, using damping and a proxy for staleness-oriented error.

Research direction

The next generation of matrix-aware optimizers will require co-design across optimization theory, numerical linear algebra, and GPU systems. The relevant target is not the fastest isolated inverse root or polar factor. It is the best end-to-end training behavior under a fixed computational budget.

Muon and the Return of Diverse Norms

Two themes stood out in the optimization conversations, posters, and workshops I encountered at ICML 2026.

1. Muon has strong momentum

There is substantial interest in understanding, improving, and efficiently implementing Muon and related spectral update rules. In the optimization sessions I visited, work centered exclusively on AdamW was much less visible than work on matrix-aware or norm-aware methods. This is a personal conference observation rather than a census, but the directional signal was clear.

2. Spectral steepest descent is reopening the norm question

Muon has helped bring steepest-descent geometry back into practical optimizer discussions. Once the update is viewed through a matrix norm rather than the Euclidean norm alone, a broader set of questions becomes natural: which norm matches a parameter block, what dual geometry the gradient should use, and how normalization interacts with scale, depth, and architecture.

This renewed interest in diverse norms is valuable because it connects implementation choices to a precise geometric object. It also creates a bridge between optimizer theory and the empirical behavior of matrix-shaped parameters in large models.

What I take from the conference

The most promising research agenda is not to replace one optimizer name with another. It is to understand which geometry is useful, which matrix function realizes it, and which numerical approximations preserve it during real training. That is the direction I intend to pursue through blockwise curvature analysis, AdamW–Shampoo comparisons, and efficient matrix-function computation.
